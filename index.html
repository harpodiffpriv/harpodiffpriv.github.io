SoK: Quantifying Privacy, Utility and Safety Tradeoffs of Differential Privacy Solutions in Cyber-Physical Systems

Keywords: Cyber-Physical Systems, Differential privacy, Autonomous Systems, Privacy-Utility-Safety trade off, Privacy, Utility, Safety

We present Harpo, a quantitative scoring framework that evaluates how thoroughly differential-privacy research in cyber-physical systems (CPS) reports along three dimensions — Privacy, Utility and Safety — across a corpus of 47 primary research papers drawn through a reproducible PRISMA-style search of 88 cited works. Harpo is a black-box scoring tool: each paper is reduced to three normalized scores in [0,1] using a Boolean reporting checklist and a fixed per-criterion weighting. Harpo scores characterize how thoroughly each paper reports along each dimension; they do not re-verify DP proofs, audit ε/δ values or estimate empirical leakage.

Applying Harpo to the corpus reveals a consistent gap: papers that emphasize privacy mechanisms largely overlap with utility techniques, while safety is rarely treated as a first-class evaluation axis. The imbalance motivates the cross-dimensional analysis that this SoK contributes.

Introduction

Why a new SoK is needed in CPS-DP.

Privacy has become important due to the rapid development of autonomous and cyber-physical systems (CPS). These systems are deployed in critical applications such as industrial control and healthcare, and they rely on continuous data exchange. Differential Privacy (DP) is a leading method for protecting that data, but integrating DP into safety-critical real-time CPS introduces constraints that go beyond the usual privacy–utility trade-off. In CPS, a privacy mechanism must also preserve system safety: task deadlines, control stability, constraint satisfaction and physical-state bounds.

Prior surveys provide valuable taxonomies but typically emphasize privacy and discuss utility mostly as accuracy degradation, while safety is treated implicitly or omitted. Harpo addresses this gap by providing a consistent, quantitative evaluation that highlights where safety-aware privacy reporting is still missing in the literature.

Safety in CPS is not a single quantity. In the surveyed literature it appears in three operational forms: (i) closed-loop stability, (ii) constraint satisfaction on physical states or inputs, and (iii) explicit hazard/risk metrics tied to collisions, failures or unsafe physical states. Harpo is designed to capture all three.

Methodology

A three-step procedure: parameter selection, weight assignment, empirical robustness. Each step is grounded in established literature so the framework is reproducible end-to-end.

Step 1 — Parameter Selection (Thematic Synthesis)

We picked the sub-criteria in Table 1 through a two-part process. Top-down, we used established frameworks as the skeleton: the NIST CPS Framework for the Safety dimension, Dwork's definition of differential privacy for the Privacy dimension, and standard control-safety treatments for the operational-safety sub-criteria. Bottom-up, we read all 47 primary research papers and recorded the evaluation evidence each paper reports; recurring patterns became Boolean sub-criteria. This follows the spirit of thematic synthesis in qualitative research.

Inclusion and exclusion rules

Inclusion: a candidate sub-criterion is included if either (a) it corresponds to a clause in NIST, Dwork's DP definition, or a recognized control-safety treatment; or (b) it appears as an explicit evaluation dimension in at least three papers in the corpus; and (c) it is operationalizable as a Boolean.

Exclusion: sub-criteria specific to a single subdomain (e.g., "PMU phasor noise tolerance"), non-Boolean-operationalizable criteria (e.g., "elegance of proof"), and duplicates of an existing sub-criterion at the same granularity.

Step 2 — Weight Assignment (Tier-Based SMARTS)

Each sub-criterion is assigned a weight using the tier-based variant of SMARTS (Edwards & Barron 1994), a standard MCDA method. Sub-criteria fall into one of three importance tiers determined by fixed rules:

  • Primary tier (weight 2k): captures scope across multiple categories within the dimension. Only P6 (Breadth of Protected Data Types) qualifies in our framework.
  • Standard tier (weight k): the default. Captures the primary form of evidence for an evaluation aspect (e.g., mechanism description, formal ε guarantee, accuracy metric).
  • Secondary tier (weight k/2): strictly weaker forms of a sibling sub-criterion (e.g., a mention versus a mathematical representation), or peripheral aspects of the dimension.

For each dimension d, the normalization factor is chosen so weights sum to 1:

kd = 1 / (2 · nPrimary + nStandard + ½ · nSecondary)

Applied to our parameter list, this yields k = 0.10 for every dimension, producing tier weights of 0.20 / 0.10 / 0.05 — the exact values in Table 1. Any researcher who applies the same three tier rules to the same parameter list will reproduce these weights.

Step 3 — Sensitivity Analysis

To verify that the ranking is not an artifact of the weight choice, we re-score every paper under two alternative weight schemes — W2 (each sub-weight multiplied by an independent uniform factor in [0.75, 1.25] and renormalized) and W3 (all Safety sub-weights scaled by 1.5× and renormalized). We compute the Spearman rank correlation ρ between the W1 baseline ranking and each alternative.

Dimensionρ(W1, W2)ρ(W1, W3)
Privacy0.99981.0000
Utility0.99941.0000
Safety0.99981.0000

The result is essentially perfect rank invariance. Part of this robustness is structural: 29 of the 47 papers receive a Safety score of zero, and zero times any non-negative weight is still zero — so those papers stay tied at the bottom under any reweighting. The Safety ranking is anchored at the bottom by the data, not by our weight choice.

Paper Selection (PRISMA-Style)

The corpus was built through a reproducible, auditable search.

Following the PRISMA 2020 guidelines, we queried IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, ScienceDirect, SpringerLink, arXiv, Google Scholar, and the proceedings of IEEE S&P, USENIX Security, NDSS and PETS. Date range: publications from 2012 through 2025 (13-year window). Searches were executed between June and July 2025.

Search query and screening rules

Boolean query:

("differential privacy")
  AND ("cyber-physical systems" OR "autonomous system")
  AND ("privacy-utility" OR "safety" OR "utility")

Inclusion criteria. A paper is included as a primary research paper if it (1) proposes, analyzes or evaluates a DP mechanism (CDP/LDP/RDP/zCDP/ε-indistinguishability/ geo-indistinguishability or a documented variant); (2) applies the mechanism in a cyber-physical or autonomous-systems context; (3) is peer-reviewed or a widely cited arXiv preprint; (4) is written in English.

Exclusion criteria. We excluded papers that (i) treat DP only in passing without a CPS contribution; (ii) treat CPS without DP;

Taxonomy of Research

The 47 primary papers are organized along three dimensions: type of protected data, application domain, and where in the system DP noise is injected.

The Harpo Framework

A Python evaluator that reads two JSON files and outputs three scores.

Harpo takes two JSON inputs: paper.json (a paper-specific Boolean checklist filled by the evaluator) and weight.json (the fixed per-criterion weights, identical across all papers). For every sub-criterion marked true, the corresponding weight is added to the total. The output is three normalized scores in [0, 1] — one per dimension.

Harpo Examples

To illustrate how Harpo scores a paper end-to-end, we walk through two worked examples.

Chen et al. [8] — ε-Scheduler for real-time CPS

Privacy
0.80 Utility
1.00 Safety
0.60

We analyze the paper by Chen et al. as an example to show how to use our Harpo framework. It proposes an ε-Scheduler that injects bounded Laplace noise into real-time task schedules to prevent scheduler side-channel leakage.

Privacy 0.80 / 1.00

The paper proposes a privacy mechanism using differential-privacy-inspired ε-indistinguishability, so the existence of a privacy mechanism, its description and its implementation are all present (P1a–c ✓). The privacy model is parameterized by ε, the sensitivity Δη and the protection horizon J and these parameters are formally used to bound information leakage through an indistinguishability inequality, so formal guarantees are explicitly provided (P2 ✓).

The paper clearly models the adversary as a scheduler side-channel attacker who observes task timing, frequencies and execution patterns to infer task behavior and compromise the CPS and this attacker model is also captured mathematically through the indistinguishability and Laplace noise formulation (P3a–c ✓).

Temporal privacy is a central part of the model: privacy loss is analyzed over multiple job instances using sequential composition up to J observations and the noise scale is derived as a function of this temporal horizon (P4a ✓, P4b ✓). As they protect both task level and job level, we consider it as multi-layer protection (P5 ✓).

Utility 1.00 / 1.00

The paper provides a comprehensive utility. Multiple performance metrics are reported, e.g., rover trajectory deviation, Kolmogorov–Smirnov statistics on timing distributions and video streaming quality measures such as bitrate and stability (U1 ✓). The protected scheduler is evaluated against a clear baseline, namely the vanilla EDF scheduler without randomization, and the results are compared quantitatively for different privacy budgets ε (U2 ✓).

Robustness is also evaluated by testing the system under different values of ε, different numbers of protected job instances and different attack scenarios — e.g., scheduler side-channel and traffic analysis attacks — and the impact of these variations on both privacy and performance is analyzed (U3 ✓).

The system is implemented and evaluated on a real cyber-physical platform, including a Raspberry-Pi-based autonomous rover and a real-time video streaming pipeline and physical trajectory and QoS results are reported (U4 ✓). More than one CPS application is considered, which provides limited but meaningful generalization across scenarios (U5 ✓).

Safety 0.60 / 1.00

The paper does not perform formal safety verification so gets no score for S1a–c. However, the system explicitly defines and enforces real-time safety constraints such as task periods, deadlines, worst-case execution times and allowable bounds on release-time perturbations to ensure schedulability (S2 ✓).

Adversarial safety is directly addressed: the paper models scheduler side-channel attacks that can lead to physical compromise of the CPS and evaluates how the proposed ε-Scheduler prevents these attacks while the system remains operational (S3 ✓).

Scenario-based safety validation is strong. The authors evaluate the protected system on a real autonomous rover following physical trajectories and on a video-streaming CPS and they report the resulting physical deviations and quality-of-service under attack and protection (S4 ✓). Real-time safety is also present, since the scheduler runs on a real-time Linux system with EDF scheduling and real hardware and physical behavior is directly measured in the experiments (S5 ✓). However, no explicit ethical, regulatory or standards-based safety framework is integrated into the design or evaluation (no score on S6a–b).

Foroughi et al. [18] — PSDO: privacy-preserving distributed optimization for prosumer energy management

Privacy
0.90 Utility
0.80 Safety
0.50

We analyze PSDO, a differentially private distributed-optimization scheme for autonomous prosumer energy management, as a privacy-and-safety heavy example with moderate utility.

Privacy 0.90 / 1.00

The paper proposes a DP-based mechanism in which Laplace noise is injected into the optimization signals exchanged between prosumers, so the existence of a privacy mechanism, its description and its implementation are all present (P1a–c ✓). The DP guarantees are stated with an explicit privacy parameter ε and a sensitivity analysis that bounds the leakage on shared optimization variables, so formal guarantees are explicitly provided (P2 ✓).

Adversarial safety is addressed through an inference-resilience analysis: the paper formally models a Bayesian-inference adversary observing noisy coupling variables across optimization iterations and shows that PSDO's adaptive noise injection keeps the adversary's posterior belief (its “Chance of Success”) low while operational constraints remain satisfied (S3 ✓).

Temporal privacy is not formally analyzed: while the optimization runs over repeated rounds, the paper does not provide a composition argument bounding the cumulative privacy budget across iterations (P4a–b not satisfied). The mechanism does combine noise injection at multiple stages of the optimization pipeline, which we consider as multi-layer protection (P5 ✓), and it protects multiple data types simultaneously — consumption profiles, generation profiles and optimization signals (P6 ✓).

Utility 0.80 / 1.00

The paper reports the optimization cost gap and convergence rate under DP noise as utility metrics (U1 ✓). The protected algorithm is compared against a non-private distributed-optimization baseline and the cost gap is quantified across multiple ε values (U2 ✓).

Robustness is evaluated under different ε values and varying network configurations and the impact on convergence is analyzed (U3 ✓). More than one scenario is considered, which provides generalization across operating conditions (U5 ✓).

However, evaluation is simulation-based on a synthetic prosumer-energy scenario and no physical CPS testbed is used (U4 not satisfied), which is the missing piece preventing a full utility score.

Safety 0.50 / 1.00

The paper does not perform formal safety verification so gets no score on S1a–c. However, operational safety constraints — voltage limits, generation/consumption bounds and stability conditions on the energy network — are explicitly defined and integrated into the optimization problem as hard constraints (S2 ✓).

Adversarial safety is directly addressed: the paper models the impact of false-data injection and inference threats on the optimization outcome and shows that the DP mechanism maintains operational-constraint satisfaction under attack (S3 ✓).

Scenario-based safety validation is provided by simulating the prosumer-energy network under attack and noise and reporting the resulting violation rates of the operational constraints (S4 ✓). However, the paper does not model real-time safety properties (S5 not satisfied) and no ethical, regulatory or standards-based safety framework is integrated into the design or evaluation (no score on S6a–b).

What Harpo Does and Does Not Do

Harpo is a reporting-completeness framework. It quantifies how thoroughly each paper documents its Privacy, Utility and Safety evaluations. It is explicitly not a DP-correctness verifier:

  • It does not re-verify DP proofs.
  • It does not audit ε / δ values.
  • It does not estimate empirical leakage.
  • It does not assess whether a paper's claimed DP guarantee actually holds under stated assumptions.

Those questions are valuable but require per-mechanism re-analysis that is beyond the scope of a scoring-based SoK. Harpo's value is in making reporting completeness comparable across a heterogeneous literature.
