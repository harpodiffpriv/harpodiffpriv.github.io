SoK: Quantifying Privacy, Utility and Safety Tradeoffs of Differential Privacy Solutions in Cyber-Physical Systems

Keywords: Cyber-Physical Systems · Differential Privacy · Autonomous Systems · Privacy–Utility–Safety Trade-off

Harpo is a quantitative scoring framework that evaluates how thoroughly differential-privacy research in cyber-physical systems reports along three dimensions: Privacy, Utility and Safety. Each of the 47 primary research papers in the corpus is reduced to three scores in [0, 1] from a Boolean reporting checklist and fixed weights. The result shows a consistent gap: papers that emphasize privacy mechanisms largely overlap with utility techniques, while safety is rarely treated as a first-class evaluation axis.

1 Introduction

Why a new SoK is needed in CPS-DP.

Autonomous and cyber-physical systems (CPS) are deployed in critical applications such as industrial control and healthcare, and they rely on continuous data exchange. Differential Privacy (DP) is a leading method for protecting that data, but in safety-critical real-time CPS a privacy mechanism must also preserve system safety: task deadlines, control stability, constraint satisfaction and physical-state bounds.

Prior surveys provide valuable taxonomies but emphasize privacy, discuss utility mostly as accuracy degradation and treat safety implicitly or not at all. Harpo gives a consistent, quantitative evaluation that shows where safety-aware privacy reporting is still missing.

Closed-loop stability

The controller stays stable under privacy noise.

Constraint satisfaction

Physical states and inputs stay within their bounds.

Hazard / risk metrics

Collisions, failures or unsafe physical states.

The three forms safety takes in the surveyed literature; Harpo is designed to capture all three.

2 Methodology

Three steps: parameter selection, weight assignment and a robustness check.

1

Parameter selection

Top-down, established frameworks give the skeleton: the NIST CPS Framework for safety, Dwork's definition of DP for privacy and control-theoretic safety work. Bottom-up, we read all 47 primary papers and logged the evaluation evidence each one reports.

A pattern becomes a Boolean sub-criterion when it (a) appears in at least three primary papers and (b) aligns with a framework anchor. P5 and P6 meet only (a) and are labeled corpus theme in Table 1.

2

Weight assignment (tier-based SMARTS)

Every sub-criterion is placed in one of three tiers by fixed rules; the tier sets the weight.

Primary · 2k

Coverage across several categories of the same criterion. Only P6 qualifies.

Standard · k

The default: the main evidence for an aspect, e.g. a mechanism description or a formal ε guarantee.

Secondary · k/2

Weaker evidence than a peer sub-criterion, or satisfied by fewer than three primary papers.

\[ k_d \;=\; \frac{1}{2\,n_P + n_S + \tfrac{1}{2}\,n_{\mathrm{Sec}}}\]

\(n_P\), \(n_S\), \(n_{\mathrm{Sec}}\): numbers of Primary, Standard and Secondary sub-criteria in dimension \(d\). For our list this gives \(k = 0.10\) in every dimension, i.e. weights 0.20 / 0.10 / 0.05 (Table 1).

3

Sensitivity analysis

Every paper is re-scored under W2 (each weight perturbed by a factor in [0.75, 1.25]) and W3 (safety weights ×1.5). Rankings barely move (Table 3). Part of this is structural: 29 of the 47 papers have zero safety and stay at the bottom under any reweighting.

Inclusion rules

Primary research paper (scored by Harpo): proposes, analyzes or evaluates a DP mechanism (ε-indistinguishability, CDP, LDP, a documented variant or geo-indistinguishability) and applies it in a cyber-physical or autonomous-systems context (data, model, control, estimation, scheduling or communication layer).

Survey, review or tutorial (used qualitatively, not scored): a literature survey, tutorial or systematization on privacy, security or DP in CPS/AS.

7 Harpo Examples

Two worked examples, then try it yourself.

Chen et al. [10] — ε-Scheduler for real-time CPS

P 0.80U 1.00S 0.60

We analyze the paper by Chen et al. [10] as an example to show how to use our Harpo framework. It proposes an \(\epsilon\)-Scheduler that injects bounded Laplace noise into real-time task schedules to prevent scheduler side-channel leakage.

Privacy: 0.8 out of 1

The paper proposes a privacy mechanism using differential-privacy-inspired \(\epsilon\)-indistinguishability, so the existence of a privacy mechanism, its description and its implementation are all present (P1a-c ✓). The privacy model is parameterized by \(\epsilon\), the sensitivity \(\Delta \eta\) and the protection horizon J and these parameters are formally used to bound information leakage through an indistinguishability inequality, so formal guaranties are explicitly provided (P2 ✓). The paper clearly models the adversary as a scheduler side-channel attacker who observes task timing, frequencies and execution patterns to infer task behavior and compromise the CPS and this attacker model is also captured mathematically through the indistinguishability and Laplace noise formulation (P3a-c ✓). Temporal privacy is a central part of the model: privacy loss is analyzed over multiple job instances using sequential composition up to J observations and the noise scale is derived as a function of this temporal horizon (P4a ✓, P4b ✓). As they protect both task level and job level we consider it as multi-layer protection (P5 ✓).

Utility: 1 out of 1

The paper provides a comprehensive utility. Multiple performance metrics are reported e.g., rover trajectory deviation, Kolmogorov– Smirnov statistics on timing distributions and video streaming quality measures such as bitrate and stability (U1 ✓). The protected scheduler is evaluated against a clear baseline, namely the vanilla EDF scheduler without randomization and the results are compared quantitatively for different privacy budgets \(\epsilon\) (U2✓). Robustness is also evaluated by testing the system under different values of \(\epsilon\), different numbers of protected job instances and different attack scenarios e.g., scheduler side-channel and traffic analysis attacks and the impact of these variations on both privacy and performance is analyzed (U3 ✓ ). The system is implemented and evaluated on a real cyber-physical platform, including a Raspberry-Pi-based autonomous rover and a real-time video streaming pipeline and physical trajectory and QoS results are reported (U4 ✓). More than one CPS application is considered, which provides limited but meaningful generalization across scenarios (U5 ✓ ).

Safety: 0.6 out of 1

The paper does not perform formal safety verification so gets no score for S1a–c. However, the system explicitly defines and enforces real-time safety constraints such as task periods, deadlines, worst-case execution times and allowable bounds on release-time perturbations to ensure schedulability (S2 ✓). Adversarial safety is directly addressed: the paper models scheduler side-channel attacks that can lead to physical compromise of the CPS and evaluates how the proposed \(\epsilon\)-Scheduler prevents these attacks while the system remains operational (S3 ✓). Scenario-based safety validation is strong. The authors evaluate the protected system on a real autonomous rover following physical trajectories and on a video-streaming CPS and they report the resulting physical deviations and quality-of-service under attack and protection (S4 ✓). Real-time safety is also present, since the scheduler runs on a real-time Linux system with EDF scheduling and real hardware and physical behavior is directly measured in the experiments (S5 ✓). However, no explicit ethical, regulatory or standards-based safety framework is integrated into the design or evaluation (no score on S6a–b).

Foroughi et al. [34] — PSDO: privacy-preserving distributed optimization for prosumer energy management

P 0.90U 0.80S 0.50
Extended · artifact-only example (not in the submitted paper)

PSDO lets prosumers solve a distribution-network energy-management problem with ADMM while protecting their private data. We use it as a second example because, unlike the Chen et al. example, it is strong on privacy and weaker on real-system validation.

Privacy: 0.90 out of 1

Each prosumer adds Laplace noise to the primal (coupling) variable it shares with its neighbors during ADMM, so the existence of a privacy mechanism, its description and its implementation are all present (P1a–c ✓). Each prosumer's privacy parameter \(\epsilon_i\) is stated explicitly together with the sensitivity of its local update (P2 ✓).

The adversary is defined explicitly as honest-but-curious: it follows the protocol but analyzes the coupling messages to infer other prosumers' private data. Its capabilities are stated and modeled mathematically through an Adversarial Bayesian Inference model that tracks the adversary's posterior over iterations and its “Chance of Success” (P3a–c ✓).

The paper does not give a composition argument that bounds the cumulative privacy loss across ADMM iterations (P4a–b not satisfied). The mechanism combines Laplace-based local differential privacy with an adversary-aware calibration layer that adapts the noise at each iteration, which we count as multi-layer protection (P5 ✓). It protects several data types at once: prosumers' generation and demand data and their private objective preferences (P6 ✓).

Utility: 0.80 out of 1

Utility is reported as optimality loss, welfare loss, number of iterations to convergence and per-node resource use (CPU, memory, power and network traffic) (U1 ✓). PSDO is compared quantitatively against standard ADMM and DP-ADMM baselines (U2 ✓).

Robustness is evaluated by varying each prosumer's privacy setting (low, moderate, high) and analyzing the effect on accuracy, adversary success and welfare (U3 ✓). Results are reported for several prosumers with different privacy preferences and generator types (U5 ✓).

However, the evaluation is a simulation with synthetic data on a modified IEEE 33-bus distribution network; no physical CPS testbed is used (U4 not satisfied), which is the missing piece preventing a full utility score.

Safety: 0.50 out of 1

The paper does not perform formal safety verification, so it gets no score on S1a–c. The physical constraints of the distribution network (power balance and line-flow limits) are defined and integrated into the optimization problem, and the mechanism is designed to return feasible solutions (S2 ✓).

Adversarial scenarios are evaluated: the adversary's inference success under PSDO is measured and compared with DP-ADMM across prosumers and iterations (S3 ✓). The threat model is passive only; the paper states that active attacks are out of its scope.

Scenario-based validation is provided by the 33-bus case study with nine distributed energy resources, reporting the resulting demand and generation dispatch and the welfare loss per prosumer under privacy noise (S4 ✓). Real-time safety properties are not modeled (S5 not satisfied) and no ethical, regulatory or standards-based safety framework is integrated (no score on S6a–b).

11 What Harpo Does and Does Not Do

Harpo is a reporting-completeness framework: it quantifies how thoroughly each paper documents its privacy, utility and safety evaluations.

It does not
  • re-verify DP proofs
  • audit ε / δ values
  • estimate empirical leakage
  • check whether a claimed guarantee holds under the stated assumptions
It does

make reporting completeness comparable across a heterogeneous literature. The questions on the left need per-mechanism re-analysis, which is beyond the scope of a scoring-based SoK.
